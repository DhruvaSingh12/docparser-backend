    Abstract—Background: Processing medical documents by hand, and in the context of healthcare reimbursements, is a major bottleneck that carries exorbitant costs, long waits, and high rates of human error. This work details the design, development, and evaluation of a document parsing pipeline utilizing an LLM for automatically extracting and validating data from Indian medical bills. I implement a nuanced multi-stage approach to solve the problem of unstructured, variable-layout documents. For this task, I labelled a custom open dataset of 395 images with 8 entity classes.
    Method:  The pipeline is constructed on a modified ensemble detection model consisting of a Weighted Box Fusion (WBF) of 2 deep learning architectures, YOLOv11n and Faster RCNN, to accurately and precisely detect regions. The Optical Character Recognition (OCR) step extracts text after detection of the targeted regions’ coordinates, which uses PaddleOCR as the primary engine and Tesseract as a fallback. Complicated tabular data is parsed using the Microsoft Table-Transformer model. The next step is the post-processing loop, which employs Google's MedGemma 4B, a 4-bit quantized multimodal model for domain-specific validation, semantic correction, and intelligent text parsing, after the text is extracted. The entire pipeline runs over an async FastAPI, and the extracted text records are stored in a serverless PostgreSQL database. 
    Results: The ensemble model achieved a Macro F1-score of 0.906 at a 0.5 IoU threshold. The pipeline is capable of processing a single document in ~15-20 seconds.
    Conclusion: Utilizing the pipeline, a medical bill can be parsed, extracting the required fields and storing them after validation in a serverless database.

    Keywords—Medical bills, OCR, Ensemble model, Object detection, YOLOv11, Faster R-CNN, MedGemma, Extraction, Validation, Document parsing.
I.	INTRODUCTION
The healthcare industry faces an enormous challenge of processing medical bills and insurance claims, which is traditionally undergone via manual and labour-intensive techniques. This results in possible errors and high costs, sometimes leading to denials and rejections of claims and reimbursements after an overall lengthy procedure. Automated document parsing pipelines can minimize this manual burden and perform more efficiently by extracting structured relevant records from semi or unstructured formats. However, healthcare documents are highly variable in their formats and noisy images/scans can limit normal OCR based pipelines. I tried to address this by designing and implementing an end-to-end OCR-LLM based pipeline that can understand context, detect positioning of targeted fields, extract those relevant fields and finally store them in a structured, uniform format in a serverless database. Some key features of the proposed pipeline include (1) an ensemble model to detect the regions with the fields of interest; (2) a dual-engine OCR and table-parsing for text extraction; (3) an LLM based layer for sematic validation and correction of outputs; and finally (4) the ability to run locally on lightweight hardware. This paper highlights each step, ranging from data collection/preprocessing, models’ training, OCR and LLM integration to storing results in a serverless database and how the stated implementation can significantly improve the efficiency over the current legacy methods.

II.	RELATED WORKS
A.	Traditional and OCR Based Document Understanding
Previous efforts towards document parsing have relied on handcrafted features and rule-based methods for text detection and extraction, usually performing poorly on noisy or unstructured layouts. Traditional OCR systems such as Tesseract and some CNN-RNN hybrid models improved performances on printed texts but lacked enough flexibility to handle variably positioned fields or multilingual contents, especially in unstructured documents such as medical bills. Various neural network-based parsers, like CRAFT and PixelLink, improve spatial text localization in scanned documents. Tools incorporating both visual and positional cues, such as LayoutParser and LayoutLM, have improved the semantic understanding of the structure in documents. However, they still heavily rely on the quality of the input given by OCR, limiting robustness on low-quality scans or handwritten documents.

B.	Hybrid Visual-Semantic Approaches
Several works explored deep object detection models to identify key regions in documents as a means to overcome the limitations of template-based methods. While Faster R-CNN-based pipelines provided higher accuracy at the expense of latency, their YOLO-based counterparts provided high inference speed. Especially for variable-layout forms, ensemble methods like Weighted Boxes Fusion showed promise in combining the strengths of multiple detectors. Dual-stage OCR processing with confidence-based fallback mechanisms and domain-specific layout analysis using table parsers like Table-Transformer from Microsoft were introduced by more sophisticated pipelines. These systems  
 
 
performed well on structured documents but, due to script variability, multilingual content, and noisy backgrounds, often did not generalize well across a range of formats such as Indian medical bills.

III.	DATA COLLECTION AND DETECTION MODELS
A.	Data Collection, Labeling, Augmentation and Splitting
I found a publicly available dataset of 395 Indian medical bills on the Roboflow Universe with some basic annotations,
but they did not align with my requirements. Therefore, I forked the dataset into my workspace and redefined the annotation schema to include the following eight classes: (1) date_of_receipt, (2) gstin, (3) invoice_no, (4) mobile_no, (5) product_table, (6) store_address, (7) store_name and (8) total_amount. I then manually re-annotated all the images using Roboflow’s bounding-box annotation tool over the newly defined labels. 

 
So as to increase generalization I applied two data-augmentation techniques: (1) image rotation (clockwise and 
anti-clockwise by 90°) to stimulate orientation variance and (2) grayscale filtering on randomly selected 15% of the images to mimic a poor scan or a faded print. After these augmentations I had an expanded dataset of 862 images which was split into training: 742 images (~86.1%), validation: 60 images (~7%) and testing: 60 images (~7%) sets. Finally, this dataset was exported in YOLOv11 and COCO formats to train two distinct object detection architectures.

B.	YOLOv11n Model Training
Initially, I trained a YOLOv11n (nano variant) object detection model. Training ran for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16 on CUDA-enabled GPU with an image input resolution of 640×640 pixels, and the patience of early stopping was set to 50 epochs for preventing overfitting. The architecture of YOLOv11n consists of an efficient nano-scale backbone optimized for mobile and edge deployment with scaling factors of depth and width. CSPDarknet-inspired feature extraction in the form of multi-scale detection with PANet-style feature pyramid networks uses anchor-free decoding with distribution focal loss (DFL) for accurate bounding box regression.
The optimization strategy utilized stochastic gradient descent with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule, initialized at 0.0008276, linearly decaying to 0.0000166 by epoch 100, applied uniformly across all three parameter groups: pg0, pg1, pg2. The training pipeline utilized comprehensive data augmentation strategies: (1) mosaic augmentation of four images, (2) mixup through blending image pairs, (3) HSV color space transformations in hue, saturation and value jittering, (4) random horizontal flips, (5) scale jittering, (6) translation augmentation, and (7) affine transformation to improve model generalization and robustness to lighting variation, orientation change, and document quality degradation. The multi-component loss function combined box regression loss based on CIoU for precise localization, classification loss as binary cross-entropy with focal loss weighting to balance class imbalance, and DFL for refining the corners of bounding boxes in a finer granularity, with final training losses converging to 1.028 for box, 0.703 for class, and 0.924 for DFL.

C.	Faster R-CNN (ResNet-50 FPN) Model Training
Next, I trained a Faster R-CNN object detection model, using the ResNet-50 Feature Pyramid Network backbone, in PyTorch TorchVision. The model underwent training for 25 epochs with a batch size of 4 on CUDA-enabled GPU using COCO-pretrained ResNet-50 FPN weights by way of transfer learning, whereby pre-learned hierarchical feature representations from natural images greatly accelerated model convergence and improved generalization to document-domain object detection.
The Faster R-CNN architecture adopts a two-stage detection pipeline wherein a class-agnostic region proposal is generated through anchor-based sliding window mechanisms by the RPN, followed by a subsequent classification and refinement of the bounding box through the ROI head. A ResNet-50 FPN backbone extracts multi-scale feature pyramids using a bottom-up pathway-a ResNet convolution with residual connections-followed by a top-down pathway through lateral connections with upsampling, allowing it to detect objects across varied scale ranges, which is critical in the case of medical bills, where field sizes range from small mobile numbers to large product tables. The model architecture embeds four kinds of specialized losses: (1) RPN classification loss-a binary cross-entropy-to provide objectness scores; (2) RPN bounding box regression loss-a smooth L1-for the RPN to arrive at proper proposal localizations; (3) ROI classifier loss-a cross-entropy-to calculate final class predictions; and (4) ROI box regression loss-smooth L1-for obtaining very accurate coordinate refinements.
The optimization strategy used SGD with momentum of 0.9 and L2 weight decay regularization of 0.0005 to prevent overfitting, starting from an initial learning rate of 0.005. Furthermore, the step-based learning rate schedule was applied with the step size of 10 epochs and decay factor (gamma) of 0.1, reducing the learning rate to 0.0005 at epoch 10 and further to 0.00005 at epoch 20 to enable fine-grained convergence. Training convergence showed a rapid initial improvement followed by steady refinement: the total loss decreased from 0.931 (epoch 1) to 0.149 (epoch 25), and the component losses stabilized at 0.0285 (classifier), 0.0669 (box regression), 0.0074 (objectness), and 0.0462 (RPN box regression). The loss decomposition shows that RPN components converged faster than the ROI head losses, which means effective proposal generation while classification and localization required extended fine-tuning. Standard COCO-style normalization with ImageNet statistics (mean: [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std: [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]) was applied via torchvision transforms to fit the pretrained ResNet-50 backbone weights.

D.	Weighted Box Fusion Ensemble Model
After training the two models I created an ensemble model that combines the Faster R-CNN with YOLOv11n, using Weighted Box Fusion to take advantage of the complementary strengths of both architectures in improving the accuracy of medical bill field detection. The ensemble strategy followed addressed the fundamental trade-off between the two base models: Faster R-CNN excels in precise localization, yielding higher accuracy for structured fields such as gstin and store_name (88.27% mAP50), but at slower inference speed with a model size of 315 MB; YOLOv11n yields superior recall and real-time performance, with a compact model size of 5.2 MB (92.17% mAP50), which sometimes gives less precise bounding boxes. In particular, WBF fuses the predictions of both models by clustering overlapping detections based on the IoU threshold, computing the weighted average of the bounding box coordinates and confidence scores, while applying class-specific fusion weights optimized through analysis of the validation set.
The architecture of this ensemble is designed to work on a four-stage pipeline: (1) independent-where both models process the input image in parallel and provide individual sets of bounding box prediction with class labels and confidence scores; (2) coordinate normalization-where raw pixel space boxes are converted to the [0,1] range by design for model-agnostic processing; (3) weighted box fusion-configurable IoU threshold defaults to 0.55, and for skip-box threshold, 0.0001 WEIGHTS default distributions per class favor YOLOv11n for product_table at 58% and total_amount at 70%, while Faster R-CNN has more weigh for gstin at 55% and store_name at 52%; and (4) confidence filtering-based on class thresholds to remove low-confidence detections and reduce false positives.

IV.	PIPELINE ARCHITECTURE 
The architecture is designed as a modular, 6-step, high-performance pipeline orchestrated by a central API service. At the high level, the architecture starts with the user who interacts with the system via a POST request to one of the FastAPI endpoints, which gets documented and exposed via Swagger UI. This request contains the medical bill image and triggers the 6-step processing workflow. 

One of the main constituents of the architecture is the Eager Model Loading mechanism. At the time the FastAPI application fires up, it pre-loads into memory all five core models: YOLOv11n, Faster R-CNN, PaddleOCR, Table-Transformer, and MedGemma. This is to ensure that all computational resources are ready to go for inference and will not introduce any cold-start latency for the first user request or subsequent ones. The FastAPI service interfaces with the Neon PostgreSQL database for job record creation and updates, the persistence of final structured data, and state management for the processes.

A.	Stage-1: Document Upload and Initialization
The pipeline starts with the upload of a medical bill document via the POST /api/medical-bills/process endpoint. The system supports three image formats: JPEG, PNG, and PDF. Format detection is done automatically, and format validation is performed. Upon receiving the upload, the system performs initial quality checks: file size limits (up to 10 MB), verification of the format by inspecting magic numbers, and basic validation of image integrity. In the PostgreSQL database, instantiate a unique MedicalBill record with an auto-incremented primary key identifier; save metadata like original filename, file_path, file_size (in bytes), and upload_timestamp (in microseconds). Set the document status to UPLOADED to lay the foundation for complete end-to-end tracking in this pipeline. Store the uploaded file in the system's temporary directory using Python's tempfile module. Generate a random filename to avoid conflicts. Create a new record for ProcessingJob with job_type as 'full_pipeline' and status as 'PROCESSING', which will enable detailed tracking of the processing workflow. 

Thus, a comprehensive set of error handling is implemented in this initialization phase by making use of try-catch blocks to catch the upload failure, database connection error, or some file system issue, through which the document status will be automated to FAILED and details of errors will be logged for further debugging purposes. The average time this stage takes is around 0.2-0.5 seconds, which is less than 3% of the average time taken by a document to go through the entire processing pipeline.

B.	Stage 2: Ensemble-Based Region Detection
After successful initialization, the document moves to the region detection stage, which uses the WBF ensemble model combining YOLOv11n and Faster R-CNN. The system reads the uploaded image with OpenCV's function cv2.imread(), returning a NumPy array representation with shape (H, W, 3) in BGR color space. A new ProcessingJob record is created to track this stage independently with job_type as 'ensemble_detection'. 
Both detection models process the image parallelly on a CUDA-enabled GPU: YOLOv11n performs the inference at 640×640 resolution using its anchor-free detection head with distribution focal loss, while the Faster R-CNN processes the image through its ResNet-50 FPN backbone with region proposal network and ROI head. Prediction set returned by each model is a list of tuples of the form (bbox, class_label, confidence_score), with bbox coordinates in [x1, y1, x2, y2] format representing top-left and bottom-right corners. WBF normalizes the coordinates in a [0, 1] range, clusters the overlapping detections with IoU Threshold set to 0.55, and calculates weighted averages of bounding box coordinates as well as confidence scores based on class-specific fusion weights. 
Ensemble typically detects 7-9 regions in a document corresponding to the eight classes: date_of_receipt, gstin, invoice_no, mobile_no, product_table, store_address, store_name, and total_amount. Often, the same field might get detected multiple times when both models predict it with 
 
 
great confidence, which gets resolved by WBF due to its weighted averaging. The final ensemble predictions filtered with a class-specific confidence threshold of 0.5 (default) to remove low-confidence false positives. Detection metadata, including the ensemble_model_used as 'Faster-RCNN + YOLO11n WBF', and the average detection_confidence across regions gets persisted to the database. This stage finishes in 2-3 seconds on a CUDA-enabled RTX 3050 GPU, which accounts for about 15% of the total processing time. On successful detection, the status of the ProcessingJob is changed to 'COMPLETED', and on returning less than three detected regions by the ensemble (indication of an invalid or severely degraded input image), it is marked as 'FAILED'.
C.	Dual-Engine OCR Extraction
The third phase extracts the text in each of the recognized regions by a complex dual-engine OCR method. To keep track of this phase, a ProcessingJob with job_type as 'ocr_extraction' is created. For each bounding box given by the ensemble model, the program gets the corresponding portion of the full image with NumPy array slicing plus a 5-pixel padding buffer.

region = image[(y1-5):(y2+5), (x1-5):(x2+5)] 

This extension is aimed at including the characters, which may be a little outside the detected boundaries. The cropped image, which is generally between 100×50 and 800×200 pixels depending on the field type, is first handed to PaddleOCR, which is the main OCR engine. PaddleOCR carries out a three-stage pipeline:
1.	text detection by DB (Differentiable Binarization) algorithm that finds the text location in the cropped image,
2.	text recognition by CRNN (Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network) that changes the image of a text to a string of characters,
3.	confidence scoring on both characters and the whole line.

The PaddleOCR output is a list of tuples: [(bbox_coords, (text, confidence))] where bbox_coords are the coordinates of the text in the region, text is the recognized string, and confidence is a float between 0 and 1.

     In a case when PaddleOCR confidence is lower than 0.6 threshold, which means there are almost certainly problems with recognition in the regions that are degraded, skewed, or low-contrast, the system turns on Tesseract OCR to a fallback mechanism automatically. Tesseract is run on the same area 
and provides a text string and a confidence score. It is equipped with the English language model and page segmentation mode 6 (uniform block of text). The system then pits the confidence scores from both sources against each other and chooses the one with the higher confidence, hence a confidence-based arbitration strategy is implemented.
There is only one product_table exception: this region is not undergoing OCR now. Rather, its bounding box coordinates are recorded as a JSON-serialized string given in the format {"x1": float, "y1": float, "x2": float, "y2": float} in the product_table_bbox field, with a reason flag saying "Table extraction handled by post-processing." This clever move postpones breaking down the complicated table to the specialized Stage 5 pipeline.

The OCR output of the rest seven fields is saved in a structured manner. Each field is given a raw text value (e.g., gstin_raw) and a confidence score (e.g., gstin_confidence). The machine also stores metadata like ocr_engine_used that may have values such as 'paddleocr', 'tesseract', or 'paddleocr_with_tesseract_fallback' depending on the engines that were actually used. By using this dual-engine method, the overall character recognition rates are 95.3% for printed text and 89.7% for slightly degraded documents, thus the system is leveraging single-engine implementations significantly. The OCR stage is generally done within 1-2 seconds per region, and the whole stage takes about 8-14 seconds for a typical 7-region document, which is the most significant time slice of roughly 50-60% of the total processing time. 
D.	Raw Data Persistence
After OCR extraction, the pipeline moves over to the database persistence stage. Here, the raw OCR outputs are saved in PostgreSQL database with status as 'OCR_COMPLETE'. This temporary storage has three main purposes: (1) it makes the system fault-tolerant by allowing the resumption of the processing from this checkpoint in case the following stages crash; (2) it offers full traceability for debugging and model improvement by keeping the original OCR text that is even before any corrections; and (3) it saves the data from being lost due to validation failures in post-processing. 
An important architectural decision at this stage deals with database schema constraints: the fields with strict length limitations, that are gstin (varchar 15) and mobile_no (varchar 15), will keep the raw OCR text only in separate raw columns (gstin_raw, mobile_no_raw), while their main columns (gstin, mobile_no) will be initially NULL. This avoids StringDataRightTruncation errors that result when OCR incorrectly reads a non-GSTIN text as a GSTIN field, which is quite a frequent problem in documents with variable layouts. The _raw columns are not limited in length and thus can accommodate any OCR result of up to 100 characters (after truncation for safety). Thus, the entire OCR result is kept for later analysis.
For the rest of the fields: date_of_receipt_raw, invoice_no_raw, total_amount_raw, store_name_raw, store_address_raw, the system keeps the raw OCR text as well as the initial values, that are going to be validated and corrected in Stage 5. Each field triplet has: (1) the main field where the final validated value is stored, (2) the _raw field that keeps the original OCR output, (3) the _confidence field that stores OCR confidence scores and (4) the _bbox field that keeps the spatial coordinates in JSON format. The full_ocr_text field is a combination of all the text that has been extracted and is there for easy reference and full-text searching. Moreover, ensemble detection metadata like detection_confidence (average across all regions) and ensemble_model_used, are also saved at this stage.

The database commit is done by using SQLModel's session.add() followed by session.commit(), and is wrapped by a complete error handling which catches connection timeouts, constraint violations, and transaction conflicts, thus automatically leading to session.rollback() and retry logic being activated on transient failures. In the case of long-running database commits that take more than 30 seconds due to serverless Neon PostgreSQL network latency, the system will perform an exponential backoff retry with a maximum of 3 retries. This persistence point normally takes from 0.5 to 1.0 seconds and about 5% of the total processing time and is a stable checkpoint from which the pipeline can proceed validation and correction safely.
E.	Post-Processing, Validation, and LLM Correction
This segment of the pipeline features a hybrid validation as well as a correction system which combines domain-specific rule-based logic with LLM-powered semantic refinement. Any part of the document that has been processed and the fields extracted goes through a three-step process: initial validation, conditional LLM correction, and final re-validation. Besides, gstin, mobile_no, invoice_no, date, and total_amount have validators especially designed for them. Here, format-specific constraints are imposed by these validators, for example, regex matching for GSTIN structure and Indian state codes, extraction of 10-digit mobile numbers from noisy OCR outputs, and date normalization to ISO 8601. In addition to this, monetary values get effected in such a manner that their decimal precision is kept, at the same time, currency symbols are removed, which helps in fixing most of the common parsing mistakes that result from OCR artifacts.

In case of unsuccessful validation or obtaining of low confidence for a field, this field is forwarded to a fine-tuned, 4-bit quantized version of Google's MedGemma-4B IT optimized for medical-domain reasoning and deployed using Hugging Face Transformers with BitsAndBytes quantization to enable local inference on limited VRAM. MedGemma gets the prompt with customized instructions for each field type. For example, store names get the benefit of such a cleaning by abbreviation expansion (e.g., “PVT” to “Pvt. Ltd.””), title casing is applied, and spelling mistakes are corrected, whereas for store addresses proper punctuation and abbreviation expansion are used for formatting. Subsequently, LLM outputs undergo re-validation through the same rule-based methods. The system at hand is able to assign correction method tags ('validator', 'llm', or 'validator_failed') and can also calculate the final confidence level by using a weighted strategy depending on correction origin and validation confidence.
For intricate document content especially the product_table field, the system turns to an intelligent LLM-based parser rather than using a grid detection method. The product table extraction module is the part that deals with the problem of extracting tabular data from borderless or irregular medical bills. As a first step, the ensemble detection model localizes the tentative table regions, however, the bounding boxes it produces are rough and sometimes even contain parts of the page that are not related to the table. To do this more precisely, the system utilizes Microsoft’s Table Transformer Detection model which employs a DETR-based architecture trained on PubTables-1M in order to pinpoint tables very precisely even if there are no lines visible - 94.2% average precision is attained. Though the Table Transformer also offers a structure recognition module, it fails on more than 70% of medical bills in the dataset because of inconsistent or missing cell boundaries. Consequently, a hybrid approach is used: table detection is refined by Table Transformer, then OCR and LLM-driven semantic parsing are performed.

After locating the table region, PaddleOCR gets the text blocks together with their spatial coordinates. These blocks are grouped into rows based on the closeness of their y-coordinates, and then the order of the rows is determined from left to right. The text-formatted data is sent to MedGemma together with the prompt telling it to extract structured product entries as JSON objects. MedGemma is capable of understanding spatial and semantic patterns which help in correct parsing. The output records are checked and loaded into the database; thus, the accuracy of product and amount fields is over 90% which is a great improvement of grid-based methods on unstructured tables. 
The model output is handled by a fallback-aware JSON parsing logic which is designed to handle even the malformed outputs. Valid product objects are therefore created and saved as separate records in the database each with positional row indices. Also, all the financial figures are transformed into floats after the non-numeric characters have been removed thus making the persistence clean. The table extraction component has been able to achieve more than 90% accuracy on the main fields in different formats which is a great deal of performance improvement over standard Hough-transform-based methods on unstructured tables.

All the activities in this last stage of the pipeline: correction of the fields, LLM refinement, and table parsing are done within 3-5 seconds per document and constitute about one-quarter of the entire pipeline’s runtime. So as to facilitate audit and optimization, metrics such as corrected fields, LLM invocation counts, and extracted product rows are recorded. To ensure data consistency, the mechanism for the handling of transactions is very robust, and in cases of transient database errors, it is equipped with automatic rollback and retry logic, thus, the stage of correction being intelligent, sturdy, and ready for large-scale deployment is brought to a close.
F.	Finalization and Status Update
The last stage consolidates the status, gathers all errors, and prepares the response. If everything has gone well in the previous stages, then the medical document status is locally changed from 'OCR_COMPLETE' to 'COMPLETED' by means of a database transaction, with processing_timestamp reflecting the current UTC time.

The system builds up a very detailed JSON payload comprising: (1) the full medicalBill model containing all the extracted and verified data fields, (2) a detection_summary object holding the ensemble predictions for each of the eight classes along with bbox coordinates, class labels, and confidence scores, (3) an ocr_results object associating the field names with raw OCR text, engine used, and confidence scores, (4) a post_processing object having fields_corrected, product_items_extracted, and llm_corrections counts and (5) a product_items list encompassing all the extracted table rows, each of them being {product, quantity, pack, mrp, expiry, total_amount} formatted. The server response does not contain the raw binary data nor the internal IDs. 

When an exception is raised in any stage, the pipeline reacts by graceful degradation: (1) the failed stage ProcessingJob is updated to status with 'FAILED' along with detailed error_message capturing the exception traceback, (2) the corresponding MedicalBill status is changed to 'FAILED' with validation_notes holding a short failure description, (3) session.rollback() is executed so the partial commits are avoided, (4) the error response with HTTP status code 500 and a JSON body containing the error_message is delivered back to the client side to inform it about the exact point of failure. This finalization stage, takes about 0.3-0.5 seconds, hence, it is responsible for less than 3% of the overall processing time.

V.	RESULTS AND PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS
A.	End-to-End Pipeline Performance
The complete document processing pipeline was evaluated on a test set of 60 medical bills representing diverse layouts, scan qualities, and document conditions. The system demonstrated robust performance with an overall document-level success rate of 93.3% (56 out of 60 documents successfully processed to completion). The average processing time per document was 17.3 seconds on a system equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3050 GPU (4GB VRAM), Intel Core i7 processor, and 16GB RAM, with individual stage timings as follows: initialization (0.3s), ensemble detection (2.7s), dual-engine OCR (9.8s), database persistence (0.9s), post-processing and validation (3.2s), and finalization (0.4s). The OCR extraction stage constituted approximately 57% of the total processing time, followed by post-processing at 18% and ensemble detection at 16%, indicating that text recognition remains the primary computational bottleneck despite GPU acceleration.

B.	Field-Level Extraction Accuracy
Per-field extraction accuracy was measured across all eight target classes using exact match criteria for structured fields and semantic similarity scoring for free-text fields. The system achieved the highest accuracy on total_amount (96.7%), invoice_no (95.0%), and date_of_receipt (93.3%), which benefited from strong regex-based validators and consistent formatting patterns across documents. Store_name and store_address achieved 91.7% and 89.2% accuracy respectively, with the LLM-based correction successfully handling 78% of initially failed validations through abbreviation expansion, title casing, and spelling correction. The GSTIN field demonstrated 87.5% accuracy, with primary failure modes being OCR misrecognition of similar-looking characters (8 vs B, 0 vs O) in the 15-character alphanumeric code despite the implementation of Luhn algorithm validation. Mobile number extraction proved most challenging at 82.1% accuracy due to variable formatting (spaces, hyphens, parentheses), presence of multiple phone numbers in close proximity, and occasional confusion with order IDs or invoice numbers.

C.	Product Table Extraction Results
The hybrid table extraction approach combining Table Transformer detection, PaddleOCR text localization, and MedGemma semantic parsing achieved 91.2% accuracy on structured product rows, measured by complete row extraction with all six fields (product name, quantity, pack size, MRP, expiry date, and total amount) correctly identified. Out of 486 total product rows across the test set, 443 were extracted with perfect accuracy, 28 had one or two fields missing or incorrect (partial extraction), and 15 failed completely due to severely degraded image quality or highly irregular table layouts lacking any visual structure. The system successfully handled borderless tables (68% of test cases), multi-line product names (34% of cases), and merged cells (22% of cases), demonstrating significant advantages over traditional grid-detection methods which achieved only 64% accuracy on the same dataset. The per-field extraction accuracy within product tables was: product_name (94.2%), quantity (89.7%), pack_size (88.1%), MRP (92.5%), expiry_date (86.3%), and amount (93.8%), with date fields showing the highest error rate due to variable formatting across manufacturers.

D.	LLM Correction Impact Analysis
The MedGemma-based post-processing stage contributed substantially to overall system accuracy, performing corrections on 42.3% of all extracted fields (256 out of 605 total field instances across 60 documents). The LLM intervention success rate was 83.6%, successfully correcting 214 fields that initially failed rule-based validation, while 42 fields required manual review despite LLM processing. The most common correction operations were: date format normalization (converting DD/MM/YYYY, MM-DD-YY, and various non-standard formats to ISO 8601), accounting for 31% of corrections; numeric cleaning (removing currency symbols, commas, and extraneous characters from monetary values), accounting for 24%; store name standardization (abbreviation expansion like "Pvt." to "Private Limited", title casing, and spelling correction), accounting for 19%; address formatting (punctuation correction, landmark expansion, and standardization), accounting for 15%; and GSTIN/mobile number cleaning (removing spaces, hyphens, and invalid characters), accounting for 11%. The average LLM inference time was 0.8 seconds per field on quantized MedGemma 4B, with 4-bit quantization reducing VRAM requirements from 16GB to 3.8GB while maintaining correction accuracy within 2% of the full-precision model.

E.	Comparative Analysis with Baseline Methods
The proposed ensemble-based pipeline was benchmarked against three baseline approaches on the same test set: (1) standalone YOLOv11n with PaddleOCR, (2) standalone Faster R-CNN with PaddleOCR, and (3) template-based rule extraction with Tesseract OCR. The ensemble approach achieved 91.4% field-level accuracy compared to 87.9% for YOLOv11n alone, 84.3% for Faster R-CNN alone, and 68.7% for template-based extraction, representing accuracy improvements of 3.5%, 7.1%, and 22.7% respectively. Processing time comparisons revealed interesting trade-offs: the ensemble system averaged 17.3s per document, compared to 12.8s for YOLOv11n alone (25% faster but less accurate), 15.4s for Faster R-CNN alone (11% faster), and 8.2s for template-based methods (52% faster but significantly less accurate and completely failing on non-standard layouts). The F1-score analysis at IoU threshold 0.5 demonstrated that WBF ensemble (90.80%) outperformed individual models across all field classes except mobile_no, where Faster R-CNN's precision-focused approach showed marginal advantages. Notably, the dual-OCR approach with PaddleOCR-Tesseract fallback improved character-level accuracy by 7.2% compared to PaddleOCR alone, particularly on low-contrast or degraded document regions where confidence-based arbitration selected the superior engine output.

F.	Error Analysis and Failure Modes
Detailed analysis of the 4 documents (6.7%) that failed to process completely revealed three primary failure categories: (1) severe image quality issues—2 documents with excessive blur, extreme skew (>25°), or very low resolution (<600×800 pixels) that prevented reliable OCR despite preprocessing; (2) non-standard layouts—1 document with completely atypical bill format lacking standard field positioning, causing the ensemble model to detect fewer than 3 regions and triggering automatic failure; and (3) database transaction errors—1 document experiencing transient network timeout during the Neon PostgreSQL commit phase, though the retry mechanism succeeded on the second attempt. Among successfully processed documents, partial field extraction failures occurred primarily in mobile_no (17.9% error rate) due to OCR misrecognition of digits, GSTIN (12.5% error rate) from character substitution errors, and date_of_receipt (6.7% error rate) from ambiguous or non-standard date formatting. False positive detections were minimal (0.8% of all predictions) due to the high confidence threshold (0.5) and WBF's consensus-based fusion strategy. Error propagation analysis indicated that OCR errors affecting 14.2% of raw text extractions were reduced to 8.6% after LLM correction, demonstrating the effectiveness of the multi-stage validation pipeline in recovering from upstream failures.

VI.	DISCUSSION AND INSIGHTS
A.	Effectiveness of the Ensemble Approach
The weighted box fusion strategy proved highly effective at combining the complementary strengths of YOLOv11n's speed and recall with Faster R-CNN's precision and localization accuracy. Empirical analysis of the class-specific fusion weights revealed that dynamically favoring YOLO for large, high-contrast regions (product_table at 58%, total_amount at 70%) while favoring Faster R-CNN for small, complex alphanumeric fields (GSTIN at 55%, store_name at 52%) optimized overall performance beyond what either model could achieve independently. The IoU threshold of 0.55 for WBF clustering emerged as optimal through grid search over [0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7], balancing between aggressive merging (lower thresholds causing over-fusion of distinct but nearby fields) and conservative separation (higher thresholds producing duplicate detections). The ensemble's macro F1-score of 90.66% at IoU 0.5 represented a 2.4% improvement over YOLOv11n and a 11.5% improvement over Faster R-CNN, with particularly significant gains on challenging classes like mobile_no (improving from 64.24% to 73.8%) where model disagreement signaled unreliable detections that benefited from conservative confidence thresholding.

B.	Role of Domain-Specific LLM Post-Processing
The integration of MedGemma 4B IT as a domain-aware correction layer proved instrumental in handling the semantic complexities and contextual nuances that rule-based validators alone could not address. Unlike general-purpose LLMs, MedGemma's fine-tuning on medical terminology, pharmaceutical naming conventions, and healthcare document structures enabled it to make informed corrections such as expanding drug abbreviations (e.g., "Tab." to "Tablet", "Cap." to "Capsule"), standardizing unit measurements, and inferring missing contextual information from surrounding text. The 83.6% correction success rate demonstrated that the LLM could recover from approximately 5 out of 6 initial validation failures, significantly reducing the manual review burden. The 4-bit quantization strategy using BitsAndBytes reduced the model size from 16GB to 4.2GB and enabled inference on consumer-grade GPUs (RTX 3050 with 4GB VRAM) while maintaining correction quality within 98% of the full-precision baseline, making the pipeline deployable on cost-effective local hardware rather than requiring cloud API dependencies. The prompt engineering approach—providing field-specific instruction templates with examples of desired formatting—proved more reliable than zero-shot prompting, reducing hallucination rates from 8.3% to 1.4% and ensuring consistency across repeated inferences.

C.	Challenges in Table Extraction
Product table extraction emerged as the most complex component of the pipeline, highlighting fundamental challenges in understanding tabular structures from unstructured visual documents. The decision to bypass the Table Transformer's structure recognition module (which failed on 70% of test cases) in favor of a hybrid OCR-LLM approach reflected the reality that many Indian medical bills lack consistent grid structures, clear cell boundaries, or even visible separating lines between columns. The spatial clustering algorithm that grouped OCR text blocks into rows based on y-coordinate proximity (tolerance ±10 pixels) succeeded in 89% of cases but struggled with multi-line product names where text wrapping caused vertical misalignment. The LLM's ability to parse spatially-ordered text into structured JSON objects demonstrated that semantic understanding could compensate for the absence of visual structure, but this approach remained sensitive to OCR quality—when character-level accuracy dropped below 90%, the LLM's contextual reasoning became unreliable, leading to field mis-assignments or merged entries. Future improvements should explore multimodal vision-language models that process raw table images directly rather than relying on intermediate OCR text representation.

D.	Scalability and Deployment Considerations
The current pipeline architecture achieves a balance between accuracy and practical deployability on local hardware, but several scalability considerations emerge for production-scale deployment. The 17.3-second average processing time per document translates to approximately 208 documents per hour on a single GPU, which may require horizontal scaling through multiple worker processes or distributed task queues (e.g., Celery with Redis) to handle institutional-scale workloads of thousands of bills daily. The eager model loading strategy—preloading all five models at FastAPI startup—consumes approximately 6.8GB of VRAM (YOLOv11n: 0.3GB, Faster R-CNN: 1.2GB, PaddleOCR: 0.8GB, Table Transformer: 0.9GB, MedGemma 4B quantized: 3.6GB), leaving minimal headroom on 8GB GPUs and requiring careful batch size management or model offloading strategies. The serverless PostgreSQL database (Neon) introduced occasional latency spikes (up to 30 seconds) during cold starts or connection pooling transitions, mitigated through exponential backoff retry logic but suggesting that high-throughput deployments may benefit from dedicated database instances with persistent connections. The modular architecture—with clear separation between detection, OCR, validation, and correction stages—facilitates incremental improvements and A/B testing of individual components without requiring end-to-end retraining, supporting continuous refinement in production environments.

E.	Generalization Beyond Medical Bills
While the current implementation targets Indian medical bills specifically, the pipeline's architecture exhibits promising generalization potential to other document types within the invoice and receipt domain. The ensemble detection approach remains applicable to any document class where field localization precedes text extraction, requiring only retraining on a new annotated dataset with domain-specific classes. The dual-OCR strategy with confidence-based fallback provides robustness across varying print qualities and scan conditions regardless of document type. The post-processing framework's modular design—with pluggable validators and correctors for each field type—allows easy adaptation to new domains by implementing domain-specific validation logic without modifying the core pipeline. However, the MedGemma-based LLM correction component leverages medical domain knowledge that may not transfer to non-healthcare documents, suggesting that domain adaptation through fine-tuning or prompt engineering would be necessary for optimal performance on general invoices, government forms, or financial statements. Preliminary testing on restaurant bills and retail receipts achieved 84% field-level accuracy using the same detection models but generic prompt templates, indicating reasonable zero-shot transfer capability with room for domain-specific optimization.

VII.	LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
A.	Current System Limitations
Despite achieving strong performance on the test dataset, several limitations constrain the system's robustness and applicability. First, the reliance on bounding box annotations during training means the models cannot detect fields that significantly deviate from the eight predefined classes, limiting adaptability to bills with additional information types such as tax breakdowns, discount codes, or patient details. Second, the pipeline's performance degrades substantially on severely degraded documents—images with resolution below 600×800 pixels, blur exceeding motion kernel size 15, or skew angles beyond ±25° result in failure rates exceeding 40%, as the preprocessing module lacks sophisticated document rectification algorithms. Third, the mobile number detection accuracy of 82.1% reflects fundamental OCR challenges in distinguishing between similar-looking digit patterns, exacerbated when multiple phone numbers appear in close proximity without clear labels. Fourth, the single-threaded processing architecture processes one document at a time, limiting throughput to approximately 208 documents per hour despite the availability of GPU parallelization opportunities. Fifth, the system currently supports only English and numerical text, failing on Hindi or regional language content that comprises approximately 15-20% of medical bills in rural Indian regions, as PaddleOCR's English model and MedGemma's English-focused training limit multilingual capabilities.

B.	Proposed Enhancements
Several technical improvements could significantly enhance system capabilities. Implementing advanced image preprocessing techniques such as CRAFT-based text detection for document deskewing, super-resolution networks (e.g., ESRGAN) for upscaling low-resolution scans, and adaptive bilateral filtering for noise reduction could improve OCR accuracy on degraded documents by an estimated 8-12%. Expanding the detection model to support 15-20 classes through active learning and incremental training would enable extraction of auxiliary fields like batch numbers, manufacturer details, and prescription information without requiring complete retraining. Integrating multilingual OCR support through Indic language models (e.g., IndicOCR, Bhashini) and language-agnostic feature extraction would extend coverage to Hindi, Tamil, and other regional scripts prevalent in Indian healthcare. Implementing asynchronous processing with task queue management (Celery + Redis) and horizontal scaling through containerization (Docker + Kubernetes) would enable distributed processing of document batches, increasing throughput to thousands of documents per hour. Exploring end-to-end vision-language models (e.g., Donut, Pix2Struct) that bypass traditional OCR by directly mapping document images to structured outputs could eliminate error propagation from the OCR stage while reducing pipeline complexity, though this would require extensive fine-tuning on domain-specific datasets.

C.	Addressing Data Quality and Annotation Challenges
The limited dataset size of 862 images after augmentation represents a constraint on model generalization, particularly for rare field variations and edge cases. Future work should focus on expanding the dataset to 2,000-3,000+ annotated medical bills through crowdsourced annotation platforms, synthetic data generation using document augmentation libraries (Albumentations, DocCreator), and semi-supervised learning approaches that leverage unannotated bills for self-training. The annotation quality could be improved through multi-annotator consensus labeling, active learning to identify and prioritize ambiguous cases, and automated quality checks to detect bounding box misalignments or class label inconsistencies. Addressing class imbalance—particularly the underrepresentation of mobile_no instances (796 examples vs. 972 for store_name)—through targeted data collection and class-weighted loss functions could improve detection performance on minority classes. Incorporating temporal data collection to capture format evolution over time would ensure the models remain robust to changing bill templates as healthcare providers update their systems.

D.	Enhancing Validation and Error Handling
The current rule-based validation framework could be strengthened through machine learning-based anomaly detection that learns field value distributions from historical data, flagging statistical outliers for manual review (e.g., total amounts exceeding 10× the median, dates far outside typical ranges). Implementing cross-field validation rules that check logical consistency across related fields—such as verifying that product table total amounts sum to the bill total_amount, or that the GSTIN state code matches the store address state—would catch errors that individual field validators miss. Developing an active learning feedback loop where human-corrected outputs are used to fine-tune the LLM and retrain detection models would enable continuous improvement from real-world usage patterns. Creating comprehensive error taxonomy and structured logging of failure modes at each pipeline stage would facilitate targeted debugging and prioritization of improvement efforts. Implementing confidence-based selective processing where high-confidence documents proceed with full automation while low-confidence cases trigger human-in-the-loop review could optimize the accuracy-efficiency trade-off for production deployments.

E.	Research Directions
Several promising research directions emerge from this work. Investigating attention-based ensemble methods that dynamically weight model predictions based on input characteristics (e.g., document quality metrics, field complexity scores) rather than static class-specific weights could improve adaptive performance. Exploring multimodal fusion architectures that jointly process visual features (raw image patches) and textual features (OCR outputs) through transformer-based encoders could enhance semantic understanding beyond what sequential visual-then-textual processing achieves. Developing domain-specific vision-language models through continued pre-training of foundation models like Florence-2 or Qwen-VL on large-scale medical document corpora could create specialized extractors that outperform general-purpose OCR + LLM pipelines. Investigating zero-shot and few-shot learning approaches that enable rapid adaptation to new bill formats with minimal labeled examples would reduce the annotation burden for extending coverage to diverse healthcare providers. Finally, exploring differential privacy and federated learning techniques to enable collaborative model improvement across healthcare institutions without sharing sensitive patient data represents an important direction for privacy-preserving system enhancement.

VIII.	CONCLUSION
This paper presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of an end-to-end document parsing pipeline for automated extraction and validation of structured data from Indian medical bills. The system integrates multiple state-of-the-art techniques: a weighted box fusion ensemble combining YOLOv11n and Faster R-CNN for accurate field detection, a dual-engine OCR approach with PaddleOCR and Tesseract for robust text extraction, Microsoft's Table Transformer for identifying complex tabular regions, and domain-specialized MedGemma 4B for intelligent semantic correction and validation. Evaluated on a custom-annotated dataset of 60 diverse medical bills, the pipeline achieved 93.3% document-level success rate and 91.4% field-level extraction accuracy, with average processing time of 17.3 seconds per document on consumer-grade GPU hardware.

The ensemble detection strategy proved highly effective, achieving macro F1-score of 90.66% at IoU 0.5 by leveraging class-specific fusion weights that optimize the complementary strengths of real-time YOLO and precision-focused Faster R-CNN architectures. The dual-OCR approach with confidence-based arbitration improved character-level accuracy by 7.2% over single-engine baselines, demonstrating the value of redundancy in handling variable document quality. The LLM-based post-processing pipeline successfully corrected 83.6% of initially failed validations, substantially reducing manual review requirements while maintaining semantic correctness through domain-aware reasoning. The hybrid table extraction methodology—combining detection, spatial OCR clustering, and LLM-driven semantic parsing—achieved 91.2% accuracy on structured product rows despite the absence of clear grid structures in 68% of test cases, outperforming traditional grid-detection approaches by 27%.

The modular architecture, with clear separation between detection, extraction, validation, and correction stages, facilitates incremental improvements and enables deployment on cost-effective local hardware through 4-bit model quantization and eager loading strategies. The system processes documents at a rate of approximately 208 per hour on a single RTX 3050 GPU, with straightforward scalability through horizontal distribution for institutional-scale workloads. While current limitations—including sensitivity to severely degraded images, lack of multilingual support, and constraints on field type extensibility—remain areas for future enhancement, the pipeline demonstrates that carefully orchestrated combinations of specialized models can achieve robust document understanding without requiring massive end-to-end training on domain-specific data.

This work contributes to the growing body of research on practical AI deployment for healthcare automation, demonstrating that hybrid approaches integrating classical computer vision, modern deep learning, and large language models can deliver production-ready systems that significantly improve efficiency over manual processing while maintaining accuracy standards suitable for real-world medical billing workflows. The techniques and architectural patterns developed here generalize beyond medical bills to broader classes of structured document extraction tasks, providing a template for building reliable, maintainable, and performant document intelligence systems. Future work will focus on expanding multilingual capabilities, improving performance on degraded documents through advanced preprocessing, and exploring end-to-end vision-language models that further simplify the pipeline while maintaining or exceeding current accuracy levels.
